Picture a dinner party in Athens, late in the evening, the wine already poured. The guests have agreed to take turns praising love. When it is Socrates' turn, he does something strange. He does not give his own speech. He tells them what a woman named Diotima once taught him.

Diotima, he says, came from Mantinea, and she was wiser than he was about love. She told him that love does not stop at one beautiful person. It climbs. You begin by loving one face. Then you notice that beauty in one body is a sister to beauty in another. Then you find it in a person's character, then in the laws a city lives by, then in knowledge itself. And at the very top of the ladder, if you keep climbing, you catch sight of beauty as it is in itself, not beautiful in one way and ugly in another, but simply beautiful.

That is a big claim to make at a dinner party. It means that the pull you feel toward a lovely face is the first step on a road that ends somewhere much higher. And the idea has had a long life. Boethius, writing in a prison cell, waiting to be executed, imagined Philosophy herself coming to visit him, and she sounds a great deal like Diotima. Centuries later, Thomas Aquinas would say that the beautiful is what pleases us simply in being seen. And in the twentieth century, Simone Weil would argue that beauty is one of the few things that can pull our attention away from ourselves.

So here is the question this chapter keeps asking. When something beautiful stops you in your tracks, is it only giving you pleasure? Or, as Diotima thought, is it trying to teach you something?
