# House style guide for the ear

Read by the brief, draft, edit, revise and baseline stages. The brief adds a
style section for its chapter; where the two disagree, the brief wins, since it
is the version Ian edits. Edit this file after the pilot listen.

Everything above the line of three dashes is notes for Ian and is not sent.
---

- Written for the ear: short sentences, one idea each. Signpost between sections so a listener always knows where they are and why.
- Second person and concrete examples over abstraction. Every philosopher arrives with a scene or an image: a place, a year, a person doing something.
- No lists, headings or parenthetical asides in the script. If it needs brackets on the page, it needs its own sentence aloud.
- Quotes from primary texts kept short and attributed aloud, before the quote: "Plato has Diotima say..." not a quote followed by its source.
- One narrator, warm and plain, no performed enthusiasm. No "fascinating", "incredibly", "let's dive in".
- Say a hard name once slowly, with who the person was, before using it again.
- Write out what a voice would stumble on: no abbreviations (e.g., i.e., etc., c.), no symbols, no footnote numbers, no URLs. Years as a listener would say them.
- Repeat the thread, not the sentence. A listener can't scroll back, so restate the question the chapter is chasing every few minutes in new words.
- Paragraphs are breaths: three to six sentences, one move each.
- The opening orients. A hook can come first, but within the first minute the listener knows what they are listening to: the conversation this chapter grows from, who was talking and who they are, and the question the chapter will chase. Shape it to the material; no stock formula, no "welcome to".
- The ending stands alone. Close on an image or an open question that keeps working after the audio stops. Never preview another chapter, promise more, say "where we go next" or sign off.
- Held beats. After a sentence the listener needs a moment to absorb (a turn in the argument, a striking quote, a question left hanging), put [pause] on a line of its own; the narrator holds a second of silence there. Use it rarely: about one every five minutes of audio, never two close together. More and it becomes a mannerism. It is the only bracketed mark the narrator understands; write no other stage directions.
- Where a quoted source has editorial brackets ([sic], [...], [T]he), don't reproduce them: quote around the change, or bend your sentence to fit the original words.
